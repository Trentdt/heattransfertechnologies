Equipment / Cooling Towers

Open circuit cooling towers

Most industrial heat ends up in the atmosphere through an open cooling tower. Water and air meet directly, evaporation does the work, and nothing else rejects as much heat per square foot of plot.

Modular or field erected

Open towers put circulating water in direct contact with air. A fraction evaporates, and the latent heat carried off by that fraction is what cools the rest. That is why an open tower can deliver water colder than the ambient dry bulb — the wet bulb is the floor, not the air temperature.

The choice between modular and field erected is mostly a question of size and site. Modular cells ship complete and install in weeks; field erected towers are built in place and are not limited by what fits on a truck. Somewhere in the middle the economics cross, and where that happens depends on your access, your basin, and your schedule as much as on the duty.

Cooling Towers / Modular Open

Modular open cooling towers

Factory-assembled open circuit towers, built as cells and shipped complete. Capacity is added by adding cells, so the tower can grow with the plant instead of being sized once for a load that may never arrive.

What modular buys you

A modular tower arrives as finished cells with fill, drift eliminators, fan, drive, and distribution already installed and run-tested. Site work is setting the cells, tying the piping and the power, and filling the basin. Against a field-erected tower of the same capacity that is weeks of schedule rather than months.

The other advantage is granularity. Two cells at half load run one fan instead of two, and a cell can be taken down for fill replacement or gearbox work while the rest of the tower carries the plant. A single-cell tower gives you no such option.

Where the ceiling is

Modular cells are limited by what fits on a truck and what the crane can pick. Past a certain duty you are setting so many cells that the structural, piping, and basin work starts to look like field erection anyway, and the economics flip.

Long, narrow multi-cell layouts also need attention to recirculation. Cells discharging into each other’s inlet raise the entering wet bulb above the design ambient, and the tower quietly underperforms in exactly the weather it was sized for. Orientation against the prevailing summer wind matters more than most layouts give it credit for.

Materials and configuration

Casing and basin are typically galvanized steel, stainless, or FRP. Galvanized is the default and holds up well in clean water at moderate cycles; stainless earns its cost where the water is aggressive or the tower has to last past the equipment it serves. FRP takes chemical exposure that would eat steel.

Counterflow and crossflow both show up in modular product. Counterflow packs more thermal performance into a smaller footprint and keeps the distribution enclosed. Crossflow runs a lower fan horsepower and puts the hot water basin where a person can actually see it, which matters if the water is dirty and the nozzles need attention.

Cooling Towers / Field Erected Open

Field erected open cooling towers

At some point the duty outgrows anything you can ship. A field erected tower is engineered and built on site, which takes the truck and the crane out of the sizing conversation entirely.

When field erection is the only option

Package towers are limited by road and rail clearance and by crane capacity. Very large heat rejection duties — utility condenser water, large refinery and steel plant loads — run past that ceiling. Building in place moves the limit from shipping to structure.

Field erection also solves problems a package tower cannot. An odd footprint. Construction phased around a plant that has to keep running. Tying into a concrete basin that is already in the ground and is not going anywhere.

Structure, basin, and fill

Structure and basin are usually reinforced concrete, FRP, or pressure-treated engineered wood, chosen against water chemistry and against how long the tower has to last. Concrete carries the highest first cost and the longest service life. Wood and FRP go up faster and tolerate chemistry that attacks steel.

Fill selection is where thermal performance is won or lost. Film fill gives the most surface area per cubic foot and the best approach, and it also plugs fastest on dirty water. Splash fill takes fouling and solids in stride and needs more height for the same duty. Getting this wrong is the single most common reason a large tower never makes its design approach.

What the project actually looks like

A field erected tower is a construction project before it is a piece of equipment. Foundation and basin work, structural steel or concrete, fill and eliminator installation, mechanical equipment, distribution, and then commissioning. Months, not weeks, and the schedule is exposed to weather and to site access.

That is the tradeoff. More on-site labor and a longer timeline, in exchange for a tower sized to the load rather than to a shipping envelope. We work through that comparison honestly, including the cases where several modular cells turn out to be the better answer.

Equipment / Closed Loop Cooling Towers

Closed loop cooling towers

Evaporative performance without putting your process water in the air. The fluid stays sealed inside a coil or tube bundle while spray water and air do the cooling on the outside, so you get wet bulb approach and a loop that stays clean.

Two heat transfer steps, and what they buy

A closed circuit unit is an open tower and a heat exchanger combined into one machine. Spray water approaches the wet bulb, then the coil approaches the spray water. Two steps in series means more surface and more airflow than an open tower of the same duty, and a higher first cost.

What you get back is a process loop that never sees the atmosphere. No airborne fouling, no biological growth in your equipment, no oxygen, and a treatment program that applies to a small spray loop instead of to the water running through your furnace coils or your glycol system. Where what is downstream is expensive or intolerant, that trade is not close.

Closed Loop Cooling Towers / WSAC

Wet surface air coolers (WSAC)

A WSAC sprays water directly over a tube bundle carrying the process stream. It cools to the wet bulb like an evaporative tower, keeps the process sealed inside tube, and is built to run on water that would destroy a conventional tower and heat exchanger pair.

How it works

Process fluid or vapor flows inside the tubes. Spray water is distributed over the outside of the bundle and air is drawn across it, so heat leaves by evaporation off the wetted tube surface. The wet bulb is the floor, exactly as with an open tower, which puts a WSAC well below anything an air cooled exchanger can reach on the same day.

What separates it from a closed circuit tower is the design intent of the spray side. Tubes are spaced and the spray rate is set so the surface stays continuously flooded. A flooded surface does not scale the way an intermittently wetted one does, which is why a WSAC tolerates spray water that no cooling tower would accept.

Why plants specify it

It replaces a cooling tower, a circulating pump set, a heat exchanger, and the piping between them with one machine. Fewer pieces, one approach temperature instead of two stacked in series, and no intermediate loop to treat and maintain.

The water quality tolerance is the real argument. Blowdown, plant wastewater, produced water, high-TDS makeup — streams that would foul a plate exchanger in a season are normal service here. On sites where clean makeup is scarce or expensive, that changes the operating cost picture substantially.

Typical service

Common duties are gas turbine inlet and exhaust cooling, compressor interstage and aftercooling, vapor condensing, glycol and amine cooling, and closed loop jacket water on engines. Anywhere the process has to reach close to wet bulb and the available water is not clean.

Field erection means the bundle count, tube metallurgy, and casing are set by the duty rather than by a catalog size. Stainless, duplex, and coated carbon steel bundles are all normal depending on what is inside the tube and what is in the spray water.

Closed Loop Cooling Towers / Modular Fluid Cooler

Modular closed loop fluid coolers

A factory-built closed circuit tower: the process fluid stays sealed inside a coil while spray water and air do the cooling on the outside. You keep evaporative performance and the process loop never sees the atmosphere.

The case for a sealed loop

An open tower puts your process water in direct contact with air, which means airborne dirt, biological growth, oxygen, and a full chemical treatment program on the same water that runs through your equipment. A closed circuit unit moves all of that to a small spray loop you can treat aggressively and cheaply.

That matters most when what is downstream is expensive or intolerant. Induction furnace coils, laser and welding equipment, injection molds, engine jackets, and any glycol loop you would rather not contaminate. It also matters when the loop has to hold pressure or run above atmospheric temperature.

What it costs you

Two heat transfer steps in series instead of one. The spray water approaches the wet bulb, then the coil approaches the spray water, so a closed circuit unit needs more surface and more airflow than an open tower of the same duty. Expect a larger footprint and a higher first cost for the same tons.

Coil fouling on the spray side is the failure mode to design against. Water treatment, a properly sized bleed, and basin filtration are not optional here. A scaled coil loses capacity from the outside in and you cannot get at it the way you can with a plate exchanger.

Dry, adiabatic, and hybrid operation

Most modular closed circuit units can run dry below a switchover ambient, with the spray pump off and the coil working as an air cooled exchanger. In a northern climate that eliminates winter water use and the freeze exposure that comes with it, and it removes the treatment load for a good part of the year.

Adiabatic and hybrid arrangements sit between the two: pre-cooling the entering air rather than wetting the coil directly, which keeps the coil dry and clean while still buying back some of the wet bulb advantage on design days. Which arrangement pays depends on the local climate and on what water costs at your site.

Equipment / Air Cooled Heat Exchangers

Air cooled heat exchangers (ACHE)

Finned tube bundles cooled by forced or induced draft air. No makeup water, no chemical treatment, no blowdown, no discharge permit, and no basin to freeze. What you give up is the wet bulb.

The dry bulb is the floor

An air cooled exchanger can only ever approach the ambient dry bulb, and getting closer than about 15 to 25 °F drives surface area and fan power up sharply. On a hot afternoon that puts the achievable process temperature well above what an evaporative unit reaches on the same day — the wet bulb is often 20 °F or more below the dry bulb.

That single fact decides most of these selections. Where the process can live with the higher temperature, an ACHE removes the entire water system and everything that comes with it. Where it cannot, the honest answer is an evaporative stage, or an air cooled unit doing the bulk of the duty with a trim cooler behind it.

The arrangements below differ in how the bundles are oriented and how the fans are placed. Duty, plot space, wind exposure, and what happens when a fan fails are what separate them.

ACHE / Vertical

Vertical air cooled heat exchangers

Tube bundles mounted upright with fans blowing air horizontally across them. Where a horizontal ACHE spreads out, a vertical unit goes up, which is often the difference between fitting on the plot and not.

Why vertical

Plot space in a built-out plant is the scarcest thing there is. A vertical arrangement takes the same bundle area and stands it on edge, so the footprint drops to the depth of the unit plus service clearance. On skid-mounted and modular packages that is frequently the only arrangement that fits inside the shipping envelope.

Vertical bundles also drain completely, which matters for condensing service and for anything that has to be winterized or steamed out between campaigns.

Airflow and recirculation

Horizontal discharge is more exposed to wind than an upward-firing horizontal unit. A crosswind on the discharge side raises static pressure and can push warm air back around to the inlet, and the exchanger loses approach exactly when the ambient is worst.

Orientation against the prevailing summer wind, clearance to adjacent structures, and adequate inlet plenum depth all matter more here than on a horizontal unit. This is worth settling on a plot plan rather than discovering at startup.

Where they land

Common on compressor packages, engine and generator cooling skids, small process condensers, and any retrofit squeezed between existing equipment. Also common where a unit has to sit against a wall or on a mezzanine with air taken from one side and discharged to the other.

Capacity per unit is lower than a large horizontal ACHE, so very large duties usually go horizontal or split across multiple vertical units.

ACHE / Horizontal

Horizontal air cooled heat exchangers

Tube bundles laid flat with fans below or above, air moving vertically through them. This is the default arrangement for large air cooled process duty and the one most refinery, gas plant, and petrochemical specifications are written around.

Induced draft and forced draft

Induced draft puts the fan above the bundle and pulls air through. Discharge velocity is high and points straight up, so hot air clears the unit and recirculation is much less likely. Air distribution across the bundle is more even. The fan and drive sit in the hot air stream, which limits the discharge temperature the fan and belts can take.

Forced draft puts the fan below, pushing air up through the bundle. The drive train sits in ambient air, so it is easier to reach, runs cooler, and handles higher process temperatures. In exchange, discharge velocity is low and warm air is much more prone to being pulled back into the inlet on a still day.

Bay layout and control

Large duties are split into bays, each with two or more fans. Two fans per bay is the usual minimum, so a single fan or motor failure costs you part of the capacity rather than all of it.

Capacity control is by variable speed drive, variable pitch hub, or louvers. Air cooled units are sized against a design dry bulb that shows up a handful of hours a year, which means they spend nearly all their life oversized. Speed control on the fans is generally the fastest energy payback available on an ACHE.

Selection realities

The dry bulb is the floor. An air cooled exchanger cannot approach it closer than roughly 15 to 25 °F without the surface area and fan power climbing steeply, and there is no equivalent of the wet bulb advantage an evaporative unit gets. If the process needs to come down near ambient, the honest answer is often an evaporative stage or a trim cooler in series.

What you get in return is no water, no treatment, no blowdown, no basin, and no freeze protection on a water side that does not exist. On remote sites, in water-restricted regions, and anywhere a discharge permit is difficult, that decides it.

ACHE / Multiple Fan

Multiple fan air cooled exchangers

Large bundles served by two or more fans per bay. It is how air cooled units get to real capacity, and it is also how they get partial redundancy and useful turndown out of the same hardware.

Redundancy that actually helps

A single-fan unit that loses its motor loses the bay. A two-fan bay that loses one fan holds roughly 60 to 70 percent of design capacity, because natural draft through the idle half still moves some air. For a process that can run reduced rather than shut down, that is the difference between a repair scheduled for next week and one happening tonight.

It also lets a fan, motor, or belt set be changed with the unit still in service, which on a continuous process is worth as much as the redundancy itself.

Turndown and energy

Air cooled exchangers are sized for a design dry bulb that occurs a few hours a year. Every other hour they have more capacity than the process needs. Staging fans off, or slowing them, is where the operating cost lives.

Fan power follows the cube of speed, so a fan at 80 percent speed draws about half the power. Staging a multi-fan bay down and running the remaining fans at reduced speed nearly always beats cycling one large fan on and off, and it is easier on the drive train.

Layout considerations

Fan coverage matters. Gaps between fan rings leave sections of bundle with low airflow, and those sections contribute far less than their share of the surface area. Fan-to-bundle ratio, ring depth, and plenum geometry all have to be right for the nameplate to mean anything.

Vibration switches, guarded drives, and access for each fan should be settled in the layout, not after. On a multi-fan unit the maintenance path to the middle fan is the one people forget.

ACHE / V Design

V and A frame air cooled exchangers

Two bundles set at an angle to each other, sharing one set of fans. The V arrangement fits substantially more heat transfer surface over the same patch of ground than a flat bundle does, which is why it dominates where plot space is expensive.

The geometry argument

Angling two bundles into a V puts more square feet of finned surface over the same footprint than laying them flat, and both bundles share the same fan and plenum. For a given duty that means fewer fans, less structure, and a smaller plot allocation.

A frame is the same idea inverted — apex up, air drawn in from both sides and discharged through the top. Which way up depends on whether the service is condensing, whether the bundles need to drain, and how the unit sits relative to surrounding structures.

Where it fits

Steam condensing is the classic application. Air cooled condensers on power and cogeneration plants are almost always A frame, because the geometry suits a large, low-pressure-drop condensing duty and lets condensate run down to a common header by gravity.

On process duty the V shows up wherever plot is the binding constraint: refinery and chemical plant revamps, offshore and modular packages, and any site where adding an acre is not on the table.

What to watch

Air distribution across an angled bundle is less uniform than across a flat one. The sections nearest the apex see different velocity than those at the open end, and a design that ignores it leaves surface area doing nothing.

Access is the other one. Cleaning and inspecting the inside faces of a V is harder than reaching a flat bundle, and fin fouling is what quietly takes capacity out of an air cooled unit over the years. Settle the cleaning method and the access path before the unit is set.

Equipment / Heat Exchangers

Heat exchangers

Five distinct families, each good at something the others are not. Efficiency, pressure and temperature capability, fouling tolerance, and whether the unit can be opened and cleaned — those four questions sort almost every selection.

How to pick between them

Start with the fluid, not the duty. A clean water-to-water load goes to plate and frame every time, because nothing beats its coefficient or its approach. Add pressure, temperature past a gasket limit, or phase change and it goes to shell and tube. Add solids and it goes to a spiral or a wide gap unit. Add an aggressive chemistry on one side only and a welded hybrid usually wins.

The second question is cleaning. A unit you can open and mechanically clean will hold its performance on fouling service far longer than one you can only chemically clean in place, and that difference compounds over the years. Specify for the cleaning method you will actually be able to use.

Everything on this site is sized against the same relationships — Q = U·A·LMTD·F and effectiveness–NTU. The equations reference has them written out, and the heat load calculator will solve any of them in either direction.

Heat Exchangers / Plate & Frame

Plate and frame heat exchangers

Corrugated plates clamped in a frame, gasketed so the two streams pass through alternate channels. Nothing else moves this much heat in this little space, and nothing else lets you change the duty by opening the unit and adding plates.

Why the U is so high

The corrugation pattern trips the flow into turbulence at Reynolds numbers where a tube would still be laminar, and the channels are narrow so the whole stream stays near the wall. Overall coefficients of 600 to 900 BTU/hr·ft²·°F on clean water-to-water service are routine, against perhaps 150 to 250 for shell and tube on the same duty.

True counterflow is the other half of it. With the streams passing in opposite directions through adjacent channels, there is no configuration correction to apply and approaches of 2 to 5 °F are achievable. A shell and tube unit cannot get close to that without going to multiple shells in series.

The flexibility nobody uses enough

Duty is set by plate count. If the load grows, the frame can usually take more plates without replacing anything else, provided the frame was specified with room to grow. Specifying 15 to 20 percent spare frame length at purchase costs almost nothing and has saved a lot of people a lot of money.

The unit also opens. Plates come out, get cleaned mechanically or chemically, and go back. On fouling service that is a genuine operational advantage over a unit you can only chemically clean in place.

Gaskets are the constraint

The gasket sets the limits: roughly 300 °F and 300 psi for standard elastomers, less for some. Gasket material has to be compatible with both streams, and it is consumable — plan on regasketing as maintenance, not as a failure.

The narrow channels that make the unit efficient also make it intolerant of solids. Anything larger than the channel gap blocks a channel and takes that plate pair out of service. Strainers ahead of a plate exchanger are not optional. Where the fluid is genuinely dirty, a wide gap or spiral unit is the better tool.

Heat Exchangers / Shell & Tube

Shell and tube heat exchangers

A tube bundle inside a pressure shell. Less efficient per square foot than a plate unit and far more tolerant of everything else — pressure, temperature, phase change, thermal cycling, and fluids that would destroy a gasket.

What it takes that nothing else does

Pressures into the thousands of psi and temperatures well past what any elastomer survives. Steam condensing, thermal fluid, high-pressure gas, cryogenic service, and anything where a gasket failure is a safety event rather than a leak.

It also handles phase change well. Condensers and reboilers are shell and tube for good reason: the shell side gives vapor room to distribute and condensate room to drain, and the geometry is well understood across a wide range of duties.

TEMA types and what they mean for you

The three-letter TEMA designation describes the front head, the shell, and the rear head. What it comes down to operationally is whether the bundle can be pulled and whether the tubes can be mechanically cleaned.

A fixed tubesheet unit is the cheapest and cannot be pulled; the shell side is chemical clean only, and differential thermal expansion needs an expansion joint. A U-tube unit is removable but the tube bends cannot be mechanically brushed. A floating head takes both — removable bundle and straight tubes — and costs the most. On fouling service that difference decides itself.

Getting the F factor right

A single-pass shell in true counterflow needs no correction. Anything multi-pass has some co-current flow in it, and the effective mean temperature difference drops. That is the TEMA F correction factor, and if it lands below about 0.8 the design is inefficient enough to reconsider — usually by going to two shells in series.

Ignoring F is one of the more common ways a shell and tube unit ends up undersized on paper. The equations reference has the LMTD and correction relationships, and the heat load calculator applies the correction for you.

Heat Exchangers / Spiral

Spiral heat exchangers

Two metal sheets rolled into concentric spirals, forming a pair of single long curved channels. The geometry makes it the tool of choice for slurries, sludges, fibrous liquids, and anything else that plugs a conventional exchanger.

The self-cleaning effect

Each stream has exactly one channel with no branches and no bypass. Where a deposit starts to form, the flow area at that point narrows, local velocity rises, and the higher shear tends to scour the deposit off. A shell and tube unit does the opposite — a fouling tube simply takes less flow, fouls faster, and eventually plugs while the clean tubes carry the load.

Curvature adds secondary flow across the channel, which improves the coefficient and keeps solids in suspension instead of settling. On sludge and fibrous service a spiral will hold its performance for far longer than anything else in the catalog.

True counterflow in one unit

Both channels run the full length in opposite directions. Single pass, no correction factor, no temperature cross problem. Close approaches and temperature crosses that would need two or three shells in series are handled by one spiral.

Compactness follows from that. A spiral rated for a duty that would take a large multi-shell arrangement fits in a fraction of the plot, which matters on retrofit work.

Where it is worth the money

A spiral costs more per square foot than a plate or shell and tube unit of the same area, so it earns its place on service where the others fail: digested sludge, pulp and paper stock, mining slurries, waste streams with heavy solids, and viscous fluids where laminar flow ruins a tube-side coefficient.

It is also fully openable for cleaning — covers come off both ends and each channel can be reached along its length, which is the practical difference between a unit you maintain and one you replace.

Heat Exchangers / Welded Hybrid

Welded hybrid plate exchangers

A plate pack where one stream runs through laser-welded plate pairs and the other through conventional gasketed channels. You keep plate-level efficiency and access on the service side, and remove the gasket entirely from the side that would attack it.

The problem it solves

Gasketed plate exchangers are limited by the elastomer, not by the metal. Refrigerants, solvents, hot oils, aggressive process chemicals, and temperatures past roughly 300 °F all rule out a standard gasket, which historically pushed those duties to shell and tube and gave up most of the thermal performance.

Welding the process channels closes that gap. The aggressive stream sees only welded stainless or alloy. The service stream — cooling water, glycol, condenser water — keeps its gaskets and stays openable.

What you keep and what you give up

You keep the corrugated plate geometry, so the coefficient stays in plate territory rather than dropping to shell and tube. You keep true counterflow and close approaches. And you keep the ability to open the gasketed side for mechanical cleaning, which is the half most likely to foul on cooling water.

What you give up is access to the welded side. It is chemical clean in place only. That is fine for a clean process stream and a poor choice for a dirty one, so the selection depends on which of your two streams is the messy one.

Typical service

Refrigerant condensers and evaporators, ammonia systems, hot oil and thermal fluid cooling, solvent recovery, and process duties running past gasket temperature limits. Common anywhere a plant wants plate performance and compactness but cannot accept an elastomer in the process circuit.

Pressure and temperature ratings sit well above gasketed plate and below shell and tube. Where the duty runs past that, shell and tube is still the right answer.

Heat Exchangers / Welded Wide Gap

Welded wide gap plate exchangers

A welded plate exchanger with channels several times the standard width and a smooth, largely unobstructed flow path. Built for fluids carrying fibres and particles that would bridge across a conventional plate pack in hours.

Why standard plates plug

A conventional corrugated plate channel is a few millimetres across and full of contact points where the corrugations of adjacent plates touch. Those contact points are what create turbulence and hold the pack rigid. They are also exactly where a fibre catches, another catches on it, and a channel closes.

Wide gap plates open the channel and reduce or eliminate the contact points on the dirty side. Fibres and particles pass through instead of bridging. You trade some heat transfer coefficient for the ability to keep running.

Welded, and what that means

Fully welded construction removes gaskets from the equation, so temperature and pressure limits rise and there is nothing for an aggressive fluid to attack. That suits waste and process streams that are both dirty and chemically unfriendly, which is a common combination.

Cleaning is chemical in place, or back-flushing, rather than opening the pack. On the service this equipment is built for that is usually acceptable, because the whole point is that it does not need opening as often.

Where it earns its place

Pulp and paper black liquor and white water, textile and dyeing effluent, food processing streams with pulp and fibre, wastewater heat recovery, and any duty where a standard plate unit is being cleaned so often that the labour outweighs the capital difference.

It sits between a standard plate pack and a spiral: more compact and cheaper than a spiral, more fouling-tolerant than a standard plate. Where the solids get heavy enough, a spiral is still the better tool.

Filtration / Cooling Water

Cooling water filtration

An open cooling tower is an extremely effective air scrubber. Everything the air carries — dust, pollen, insects, fly ash, whatever the plant next door is emitting — ends up in your basin and then in your equipment. Filtration is what keeps it there.

What ends up in the basin

A 1,000 ton tower moves on the order of a million cubic feet of air an hour. Even at low airborne solids loading that is a meaningful mass of dirt per day going into the circulating water. It settles in the basin, coats heat transfer surfaces, and gives biological growth somewhere to establish.

Fouling shows up as approach temperature that drifts up over the season, condenser pressure that climbs, and cleaning intervals that shorten. By the time it is obvious in the operating data, the deposit is well established.

Basin sweeping and separators

Basin sweeper piping keeps solids in suspension so they reach the filter instead of settling into a layer of sludge. Without it, the filter treats water that has already dropped its solids load and the basin needs manual cleaning.

Centrifugal separators remove settleable solids down to roughly 40 to 75 microns with no media and almost no maintenance, at the cost of a fixed pressure drop. They are a good first stage and a poor last one — the fine fraction that does the most damage to heat transfer surface passes straight through.

Sand, media, and automatic screens

Sand and multimedia filters go finer, roughly 10 to 20 microns, and backwash automatically on differential pressure or on a timer. Backwash water goes to drain, so the water and treatment cost of the backwash belongs in the operating comparison.

Automatic self-cleaning screen filters sit between the two: mechanical screens that scrape or backflush without a media bed, with far lower water loss than a sand filter. Which of the three fits depends on particle size distribution, on how much water you can afford to send down the drain, and on whether anyone is available to maintain it.

Filtration / Process Water

Process water filtration

Cooling water filtration protects a tower loop. Process water filtration protects whatever is downstream of it — and downstream is usually where the tolerance is tight, the passages are small, and the replacement cost is high.

The tolerance is set downstream

A plate exchanger with a 2 mm channel, an induction furnace coil, a laser optic, a mold cooling circuit, a spray nozzle bank — each has a particle size above which it plugs, and that number, not the pump or the piping, sets the filtration requirement.

Start there. Find the smallest passage in the loop and filter to a fraction of it. Working backward from the equipment is the only way to size this correctly; specifying a filter by flow rate alone gets you a filter that fits the pipe and not the problem.

Full flow versus side stream

Full flow filtration treats everything before it reaches the equipment, which is what you want when the downstream tolerance is genuinely tight. It also means the entire system flow takes the filter pressure drop, and a plugged element affects the whole loop.

Side stream treats a fraction continuously and is cheaper to install and operate, but it only reduces the average particle load — it does not guarantee that any given particle is caught before it reaches the equipment. For tight-tolerance duty, full flow is the honest answer.

Closed loops still need it

A sealed loop does not stay clean on its own. Construction debris, weld slag, and pipe scale come with the installation. Corrosion products keep forming for the life of the system. Seal and gasket material sheds. All of it circulates until something catches it.

On new closed loops the first weeks are the worst, and a temporary coarse filter during flushing and commissioning saves the permanent elements and, more importantly, saves the equipment.

Filtration / Side Stream & Inline

Side stream and inline filtration

Side stream filtration continuously cleans a fraction of the loop. Inline filtration protects a specific piece of equipment. They solve different problems and get sized on completely different logic, which is why they are often specified wrong.

How side stream sizing actually works

The usual rule is 3 to 10 percent of circulating flow, and it is a rule of thumb rather than a calculation. What it really depends on is how fast dirt enters the system against how fast the filter removes it. A tower in a dusty environment loads faster and needs a larger fraction than one on a clean site.

A side stream filter reaches an equilibrium solids concentration, not zero. Higher fraction means lower equilibrium. Understanding that is the difference between a filter that visibly improves your water and one that runs for years while the operators wonder what it is doing.

Taking the draw from the right place

Draw from where the solids are. The basin sump, downstream of a sweeper header, or the low point of a loop — not from a clean header where the water has already dropped its load. Return the filtered water where it will do the most good, usually to the far end of the basin or upstream of the equipment you are protecting.

Suction and return placed a few feet apart is the classic mistake: the filter recirculates its own clean water and the rest of the basin never sees it.

Inline is a different question

Inline filtration takes the full stream and takes the full pressure drop. The sizing question is not what fraction to treat but what happens when the element loads up — differential pressure alarms, duplex baskets with a transfer valve, or automatic backflush, so that a plugging filter does not become a process outage.

On critical service, duplex arrangements let an element be changed with the loop running. On anything continuous, that capability is usually worth more than the filter itself.

Describe the loop and what you are trying to protect. The answer is often both, sized differently.

Pumps and pumping systems

Pump selection looks like the easy part of a cooling system and generates more field problems than the equipment it serves. Almost all of them come from the same place: a system curve that was estimated rather than calculated.

Select against the real curve

Head gets estimated conservatively at every step, safety factors stack, and the pump is selected on the total. In service the actual system curve is lower, the pump runs out toward runout, and you get poor efficiency, high radial loading, early seal and bearing failures, and a discharge valve someone throttled halfway to make it behave.

That throttled valve is the whole problem in one image. It is converting head your pump is producing into heat, continuously, because the pump was never going to sit where the selection said it would.

Calculate the friction loss properly, be honest about static head, check available NPSH against required, and look at where the operating point actually lands — not just whether the pump covers the duty. Variable speed is worth costing on anything whose load varies, since power follows the cube of speed.

Pumps / Cooling Water

Cooling water pumps

Condenser water and tower circulating pumps. Almost every one of these that runs badly runs badly for the same reason: the head was estimated by adding up worst cases, and the pump ended up far to the right of where it was supposed to sit.

Oversizing is the default failure

System head gets estimated conservatively at every step — a safety factor on the piping, another on the exchanger, another on the tower — and the pump is selected on the total. In service the real curve is lower, so the pump runs out toward runout: high flow, low head, poor efficiency, and radial loading the bearings were not sized for.

The symptoms are familiar. Seals that fail early, bearings that run hot, noise, and a throttled discharge valve that someone closed halfway to make it behave. That valve is burning the excess head as heat, every hour of every day.

Static head on an open tower

On an open cooling tower loop, static head is the lift from the basin water level to the point of discharge in the tower — not the height of the tower. The return leg is a siphon and gives most of its head back, provided the piping is arranged so the siphon holds.

Getting this wrong in either direction is common. Overstate it and the pump is oversized. Understate it, or break the siphon with a poorly placed high point, and the tower does not get its design flow.

NPSH and the tower basin

Available NPSH on a tower loop is usually modest: the basin is close to the pump, the water is warm, and the vapour pressure is higher than people expect. Marginal NPSH gives you cavitation that eats impellers and sounds like gravel in the casing.

Suction piping deserves the same attention as the pump selection. Adequate submergence, a straight run into the suction, and no elbows immediately at the flange. An anti-vortex arrangement in the basin is cheap compared to what a vortex costs you.

Pumps / Circulating & Process

Circulating and process pumping

Closed loop circulating pumps and process duty pumping. Different problem from a tower loop: no static head to speak of, but fluid properties, temperature, and control range that a water curve does not describe.

Closed loops have no static head

A closed loop is balanced — whatever the fluid climbs on the supply side it gives back on the return. The pump sees friction loss only, plus the pressure drop of the equipment in the loop. That makes closed loop pumps lower head and, in principle, easier to select correctly.

The trap is fill and expansion. Static pressure in the loop comes from the expansion tank and the fill point, and where that connects relative to the pump determines whether the pump adds to or subtracts from the pressure at every other point in the system. Pump away from the expansion tank connection, not toward it.

Glycol changes the numbers

A 30 percent ethylene glycol solution runs about 1.04 specific gravity and roughly 0.90 specific heat, so the same duty needs more flow than water and the pump does more work moving it. Viscosity is the bigger effect — at low temperature glycol is dramatically thicker than water, and both pump performance and heat transfer coefficients drop with it.

Selecting a glycol loop pump off a water curve is a routine and expensive mistake. The heat load calculator carries real glycol property tables and will show you what the concentration actually does to the flow requirement.

Variable flow and control

Most process loops spend most of their life below design load. Variable speed on the circulating pump follows the affinity laws, so power drops with the cube of speed and a modest turndown is a large energy saving.

What has to be checked is minimum flow through the equipment. Exchangers lose their coefficient badly once flow drops into the laminar range, and some equipment has a hard minimum below which it should not run. The control strategy needs a floor, and the floor needs to come from the equipment rather than from the pump.

Chemical dosing skids

Packaged chemical injection: metering pumps, day tanks, calibration columns, containment, and the controls and instrumentation that make the dose respond to the system rather than to a timer.

Dose to the system, not to the clock

Industrial process chillers

A cooling tower can only get you down to the wet bulb. When the process needs colder than that, you need a chiller.

Process chilling is not comfort cooling

Comfort equipment is sized for a seasonal peak and spends most of the year loafing. A process chiller runs continuously, often at a fixed leaving temperature, often on glycol, and frequently in a room that is hot and dirty. Compressor duty cycles, control tolerance, and materials all differ, and a machine specified from a building catalogue will not survive the duty.

Ask what the process actually needs at the point of use, not what the loop is set to. The difference between 45 °F and 50 °F leaving temperature is roughly 10 to 15 percent on compressor power, every hour, forever. That number is worth arguing about before the machine is ordered.

Air cooled or water cooled

A water cooled chiller rejects to condenser water and therefore to the wet bulb, so it runs at a lower condensing temperature and better efficiency — but it needs a tower, a condenser water pump, treatment, and the maintenance that comes with all three. An air cooled machine rejects to the dry bulb, needs none of that, and pays for it in condensing temperature on the hottest days.

Where a cooling tower already exists and has spare capacity, water cooled almost always wins on operating cost. Where it does not, the comparison has to include the whole tower system, not just the chiller.

Sizing, staging, and turndown

Undersize the machine and it runs flat out and wears early. Oversize it and you pay twice: once at purchase, and again every month in short cycling that is hard on compressors and holds temperature worse than a correctly sized machine at part load.

Where the load varies widely, multiple smaller machines staged against demand beat one large one. You get redundancy, better part load efficiency, and the ability to service one unit without shutting the process down. That decision has to be made at specification, because it changes the piping, the electrical, and the footprint.

Glycol changes the machine

Glycol lowers specific heat and raises viscosity, so the same duty needs more flow and the evaporator sees a worse coefficient. A 30 percent propylene glycol loop needs roughly 10 percent more flow than water for the same load, and the effect gets worse as temperature drops.

It also sets a hard floor. Leaving fluid temperature below the freeze point of the solution is not a design point, it is a failure. The heat load calculator carries real glycol property tables and will refuse a leaving temperature below the freeze point rather than quietly return a number.

Ongoing support

Chillers lose efficiency quietly. Fouled condenser tubes, a refrigerant charge that has drifted, a failing sensor holding the machine at the wrong setpoint — none of it announces itself, and all of it shows up on the power bill long before it shows up as a fault.

A periodic performance check against the original design point catches that while it is still a tuning problem. We stay with the machine for its life.

Equipment / Direct Steam Injection

Direct steam injection heaters

Direct steam injection puts steam straight into the flowing liquid. There is no exchanger surface in between, which is the whole point.

How direct steam injection works

Steam enters the flowing liquid through a nozzle or sparger and condenses in it, handing over essentially all of its latent heat on the spot. There is no wall between the two streams, so there is no surface to foul, no approach temperature to design around, and no exchanger area to calculate.

Thermal efficiency runs close to 100 percent because the steam has nowhere else to put its energy. The catch is that the condensate stays in the process. Where the added water can be tolerated or is wanted, that is free heating; where it cannot, direct injection is off the table regardless of how well it would otherwise fit.

Spargers and engineered injectors

A perforated sparger in a tank is the simplest form and is often enough for batch heating where response time does not matter much. Its weakness is steam hammer: when steam bubbles collapse in liquid that is well below saturation, the implosion is violent, loud, and hard on piping.

An engineered injector controls the condensation rather than letting it happen wherever it wants. Steam is broken up and mixed into the liquid inside the device, so collapse happens under controlled conditions. That buys quiet operation, in-line mixing, and much tighter temperature control — which is why continuous, in-line duty almost always uses one.

Where it fits

Process and wash-down water heating, tank and vessel heating, CIP solution heating, slurry and mash heating in food and beverage, and any duty where response time matters more than the simplicity of an indirect exchanger.

It is also the right answer on fluids that would foul a heat exchanger surface badly. With no surface, there is nothing to foul and nothing to clean. On heavy slurries that argument can outweigh everything else.

Sizing and what to check

Sizing comes down to three numbers: liquid flow, required temperature rise, and available steam pressure. Steam demand follows directly from the first two and the latent heat at the operating pressure, and the injector is selected against the pressure you actually have at the connection rather than at the boiler.

The two things that most often get missed are backpressure and turndown. An injector needs sufficient pressure differential to work; too little and it will not mix, too much and control gets coarse. And a heater sized only for full flow will behave badly at the low end, which matters if the process runs at part rate.

Control systems and instrumentation

Controls are what make a pile of equipment behave like a system. They hold setpoint, follow the load, and tell you when something is drifting. They are also where a surprising amount of efficiency quietly disappears.

Common heat transfer equations

The relationships we reach for most often: heat balance, exchanger sizing, tower performance, water balance, and pump and fan power. A unit converter is built in. U.S. customary units throughout unless a line says otherwise.

HVAC ton vs. cooling tower ton — these are not the same unit

HVAC & refrigeration ton

1 ton = 12,000 BTU/hr

Rates chillers, compressors, and HVAC equipment. It counts the refrigeration effect only, meaning the heat absorbed at the evaporator. Written TR on this site.

≠

Nominal cooling tower ton

1 ton = 15,000 BTU/hr

Rates cooling towers. Defined as rejecting the heat in 3 GPM of condenser water cooled through 10 °F, which covers the compressor’s heat of compression as well as the refrigeration effect. Written TT on this site.

Rule of thumb, not a conversion: a typical chiller plant’s nominal tower-ton requirement runs close to its refrigeration-ton load, because the units were historically calibrated to line up for that case. They remain two different units of BTU/hr. Check which one a spec sheet, a quote, or your own calculation is using before you size anything. Heat balance

Sensible heat — general form

Q = ṁ · cp · ΔT

Q = heat transfer rate (BTU/hr) · ṁ = mass flow rate (lb/hr) · cp = specific heat (BTU/lb·°F) · ΔT = temperature change (°F)

The foundation for every other heat balance equation below. The water and air forms are this equation with density and specific heat folded into a constant.

Water-side heat load

Q = 500 · GPM · ΔT

Q = heat load (BTU/hr) · GPM = water flow (gal/min) · ΔT = temperature change (°F)

The 500 comes from 8.34 lb/gal × 60 min/hr × 1.0 BTU/lb·°F, good for water near ambient. Glycol needs its own numbers. A 30% ethylene glycol mix runs closer to 480, and using 500 anyway will undersize the flow.

Air-side heat load (sensible)

Q = 1.08 · CFM · ΔT

Q = sensible heat (BTU/hr) · CFM = air flow (ft³/min) · ΔT = dry bulb temperature change (°F)

Constant assumes standard air at sea level (0.075 lb/ft³). Correct for altitude and temperature on high-elevation or high-temperature sites where actual air density is materially lower.

Total heat — air, including latent

Qtotal = 4.5 · CFM · Δh

Δh = enthalpy change (BTU/lb of dry air)

Used where moisture is being added or removed, as in evaporative cooling. Sensible-only equations understate the true load in these cases.

Heat exchanger sizing

Overall heat transfer rate

Q = U · A · LMTD · F

U = overall heat transfer coefficient (BTU/hr·ft²·°F) · A = surface area (ft²) · LMTD = log mean temperature difference (°F) · F = configuration correction factor (1.0 for true counterflow)

The central sizing relationship. Solve for A when specifying an exchanger; solve for U when evaluating whether an installed unit has fouled below design performance.

Log mean temperature difference

LMTD = (ΔT₁ − ΔT₂) ÷ ln(ΔT₁ ÷ ΔT₂)

ΔT₁ = temperature difference at one end · ΔT₂ = temperature difference at the other end

For counterflow, ΔT₁ = Thot,in − Tcold,out and ΔT₂ = Thot,out − Tcold,in. When the two are nearly equal the log term heads for zero. Use the arithmetic mean instead and avoid the divide-by-zero.

Overall coefficient from individual resistances

1/U = 1/hi + Rfi + x/k + Rfo + 1/ho

hi, ho = inside/outside film coefficients · Rfi, Rfo = fouling factors · x = wall thickness · k = wall thermal conductivity

Resistances add in series. In industrial service the fouling terms usually dominate, which is why filtration and water treatment move exchanger performance so directly.

Effectiveness–NTU

ε = Qactual ÷ Qmax  |  NTU = U·A ÷ Cmin

ε = effectiveness (0–1) · Cmin = smaller of the two ṁ·cp products

Useful when outlet temperatures are unknown, where LMTD would require iteration. Qmax = Cmin · (Thot,in − Tcold,in).

Cooling tower performance

Range

Range = Thot water in − Tcold water out

Set by the process heat load and the circulating flow, not by the tower. A tower does not determine its own range. The system does.

Approach

Approach = Tcold water out − Tambient wet bulb

The real measure of what a tower can do. Approach never reaches zero at any finite size, because the wet bulb is a thermodynamic floor. Below about 7 °F, size and cost start climbing steeply.

Tower efficiency

η = Range ÷ (Range + Approach) × 100%

A quick relative indicator, most useful for comparing operating condition against design rather than comparing different towers to each other.

Liquid-to-gas ratio

L/G = ṁwater ÷ ṁair

A key design parameter for fill selection and fan sizing. Higher L/G means more water per unit of air, generally requiring more fill or airflow to hold the same approach.

Water balance

Evaporation rate

E ≈ 0.001 · GPM · Range

E = evaporation (GPM) · Range = cooling range (°F)

The old rule: about 1% of circulating flow evaporates per 10 °F of range. Real evaporation moves with ambient conditions, so use this for budgeting water, not for a permit submittal.

Cycles of concentration

COC = TDScirculating ÷ TDSmakeup

Conductivity or chloride ratios give the same answer. Running higher cycles saves water and chemical, and costs you margin against scale and corrosion. Makeup water chemistry sets the practical ceiling.

Blowdown rate

B = E ÷ (COC − 1) − D

B = blowdown (GPM) · E = evaporation (GPM) · D = drift loss (GPM)

Drift is typically small in modern towers with effective eliminators (often below 0.005% of circulating flow) but should not be ignored on older equipment.

Makeup water

M = E + B + D

Total makeup equals everything leaving the system. Useful for sizing makeup lines and estimating annual water cost.

Flow, pressure & power

Pump brake horsepower

BHP = (GPM · H · SG) ÷ (3960 · η)

H = total head (ft) · SG = specific gravity · η = pump efficiency (decimal)

The 3960 constant converts to horsepower for water. Multiply by motor efficiency to get electrical input power for energy cost estimates.

Fan brake horsepower

BHP = (CFM · SP) ÷ (6356 · η)

SP = static pressure (in. w.g.) · η = fan efficiency (decimal)

Fan energy is a large share of what a tower or air-cooled exchanger costs to run. Worth pricing during selection rather than discovering later.

Affinity laws

Q₂/Q₁ = N₂/N₁  |  H₂/H₁ = (N₂/N₁)²  |  P₂/P₁ = (N₂/N₁)³

Q = flow · H = head/pressure · P = power · N = speed

The cube relationship on power is why variable speed drives pay back quickly on pumps and fans: a 20% speed reduction cuts power roughly in half.

Reynolds number

Re = (ρ · V · D) ÷ μ

ρ = density · V = velocity · D = characteristic diameter · μ = dynamic viscosity

Below about 2,300 the flow is laminar; above about 4,000 it is turbulent. Turbulence improves heat transfer considerably. That is why running an exchanger at low flow hurts performance more than the flow reduction alone would suggest.
