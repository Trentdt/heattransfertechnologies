Equipment / Cooling Towers

Industrial cooling towers

Cooling towers are the backbone of most industrial heat rejection systems, and getting the specification right affects everything downstream — energy cost, water use, maintenance load, and equipment life. We supply both open and closed circuit towers sized for your heat load, water chemistry, and site conditions.

Open circuit cooling towers

Open circuit (evaporative) towers reject heat through direct contact between water and air, offering high efficiency and a smaller footprint for a given heat load. We help specify towers suited to your makeup water quality, cycles of concentration, and local environmental requirements.

Closed circuit cooling towers

Closed circuit towers keep process fluid isolated in a closed loop, reducing fouling and contamination risk for sensitive equipment. These systems are common where water quality is a concern or where process fluid can’t be exposed to open-air conditions.

Equipment / Closed Loop Coolers

Closed loop coolers

Closed loop coolers combine the heat rejection of a cooling tower with the fluid isolation of a closed system — protecting compressors, engines, and process equipment from the fouling, scaling, and corrosion that come with open recirculating water.

How closed loop systems protect your equipment

By keeping process fluid sealed in a closed circuit and rejecting heat through an external spray or air-cooled section, closed loop coolers dramatically reduce maintenance load on downstream equipment. This is especially valuable where unplanned downtime is expensive or where water treatment on an open system would be costly.

Common applications

Closed loop coolers are widely used for engine jacket water cooling, air compressor cooling, quench systems, and process equipment cooling across Power, Steel, and Process facilities.

Specification support

We work with your plant conditions, heat load, and water quality data to specify a closed loop system that fits the application — and stay involved through startup and ongoing performance monitoring.

Industrial process chillers

Where ambient cooling isn’t enough — or where process temperatures need to run below what a cooling tower alone can achieve — chillers provide the additional capacity your process demands.

Process chilling vs. comfort cooling

Process chilling has different demands than building comfort cooling: tighter temperature control, continuous duty cycles, and often more aggressive fluid chemistry. We make sure the chiller you specify is built for the duty it’s actually going to see.

Equipment / Air-Cooled Heat Exchangers

Air-cooled heat exchangers

Air-cooled heat exchangers (fin-fan coolers) reject heat directly to ambient air, eliminating the water consumption and water treatment concerns that come with evaporative cooling systems.

When air cooling makes sense

Air-cooled systems are the right call where water is scarce, expensive, or regulated, where zero-discharge requirements apply, or where the complexity of water treatment isn’t worth the efficiency gain of evaporative cooling.

Design considerations

Ambient design temperature, footprint, noise requirements, and fan energy consumption all factor into sizing an air-cooled exchanger correctly. We work through these variables with your team before specifying equipment.

Fit for remote & water-limited sites

Oil & Gas, Hydrogen, and Carbon Capture facilities are increasingly sited where water access is limited — making air-cooled systems a growing part of our equipment mix for these markets.

Equipment / Filtration Systems

Cooling system filtration

Suspended solids, biological growth, and debris in recirculating water accelerate fouling, corrosion, and scale formation throughout a cooling system. Properly specified filtration protects the performance and lifespan of every downstream component.

Side-stream filtration

Side-stream filtration treats a portion of the recirculating flow continuously, removing solids over time without the pressure drop or footprint of full-flow systems. A common, cost-effective solution for open recirculating towers.

Full-flow filtration

Where water quality demands more aggressive treatment, full-flow filtration processes the entire system flow, offering higher removal efficiency at the cost of additional pressure drop and equipment size.

Protecting your equipment investment

Filtration is often the most overlooked component in a cooling system — and the one most directly tied to unplanned downtime. We help you specify filtration sized to your actual water quality and system demands.

Home/Equipment/Pumps
Equipment / Pumps

Pumps and pumping systems

Circulating pumps set the flow that every other component in the loop depends on. Sizing them correctly is the difference between a system that holds design conditions efficiently and one that wastes energy and wears out early.

Circulating and process pumps

We specify end-suction, split-case, vertical turbine, and specialty process pumps sized for your flow, total head, and duty cycle. Selection works from the actual system curve rather than a nameplate figure, so the pump operates near its best efficiency point instead of running back on its curve.

Sizing against the real system curve

Oversizing is the most common problem we find in the field. A pump selected with too much safety margin runs throttled, wastes energy continuously, and suffers accelerated seal and bearing wear. We work from measured or calculated system resistance to size the pump to the duty it will actually see.

Glycol changes this calculation. A glycol mix carries less heat per gallon than water, so it needs more flow for the same duty. The selection tool accounts for this at any concentration.

Redundancy and variable speed

For continuous-duty applications we help work through N+1 arrangements, alternating duty schedules, and where variable frequency drives pay back. Because pump power follows the cube of speed, a modest speed reduction on a variable load produces disproportionate energy savings. See the affinity laws for the relationship.

Equipment / Controls

Control systems and instrumentation

Controls are what turn a collection of equipment into a system that holds setpoint, responds to load, and tells you when something is drifting. They are also the most common place we find performance quietly left on the table.

Control packages

We supply and specify packaged control systems for cooling towers, closed loop coolers, chillers, and pumping systems, from single-unit controllers through panels that sequence multiple cells and pumps against a common load. Packages are specified to suit the equipment actually installed rather than fitted as a generic add-on.

Instrumentation and monitoring

Temperature, flow, pressure, level, and conductivity instrumentation gives you the data to run the system deliberately instead of reactively. Well-placed instrumentation is what makes performance evaluation possible. Without it, a gradual loss of efficiency stays invisible until something fails.

Variable frequency drives and sequencing

Fan and pump drives, staged cell control, and load-based sequencing let a system track real demand rather than running flat out. On a multi-cell tower, running more cells at lower fan speed is often substantially more efficient than running fewer cells at full speed. That is a control strategy question rather than an equipment one.

Integration with plant systems

Most facilities need cooling equipment to report into an existing building automation system, SCADA, or plant historian. We help specify controllers with the right protocol support and work through which points need to be exposed, so the equipment is visible where your operators already look.

Engineering Reference

Common heat transfer equations

A working reference for the relationships used most often in industrial cooling: heat balance, exchanger sizing, cooling tower performance, water balance, and pump/fan power — plus a live unit converter. U.S. customary units throughout unless noted.

Unit Converter Heat Balance Exchanger Sizing Cooling Towers Water Balance Flow & Power

Unit converter

Type a value into either side of any row — the other side updates as you type. Rows are independent.

Heat balance

Sensible heat — general form

Q = ṁ · cp · ΔT

Q = heat transfer rate (BTU/hr) · ṁ = mass flow rate (lb/hr) · cp = specific heat (BTU/lb·°F) · ΔT = temperature change (°F)

The foundation for every other heat balance equation below. The water and air forms are this equation with density and specific heat folded into a constant.

Water-side heat load

Q = 500 · GPM · ΔT

Q = heat load (BTU/hr) · GPM = water flow (gal/min) · ΔT = temperature change (°F)

The 500 constant = 8.34 lb/gal × 60 min/hr × 1.0 BTU/lb·°F, valid for water near ambient conditions. For glycol solutions substitute actual specific heat and density — a 30% ethylene glycol mix runs closer to 480.

Air-side heat load (sensible)

Q = 1.08 · CFM · ΔT

Q = sensible heat (BTU/hr) · CFM = air flow (ft³/min) · ΔT = dry bulb temperature change (°F)

Constant assumes standard air at sea level (0.075 lb/ft³). Correct for altitude and temperature on high-elevation or high-temperature sites where actual air density is materially lower.

Total heat — air, including latent

Qtotal = 4.5 · CFM · Δh

Δh = enthalpy change (BTU/lb of dry air)

Used where moisture is being added or removed, as in evaporative cooling. Sensible-only equations understate the true load in these cases.

Heat exchanger sizing

Overall heat transfer rate

Q = U · A · LMTD · F

U = overall heat transfer coefficient (BTU/hr·ft²·°F) · A = surface area (ft²) · LMTD = log mean temperature difference (°F) · F = configuration correction factor (1.0 for true counterflow)

The central sizing relationship. Solve for A when specifying an exchanger; solve for U when evaluating whether an installed unit has fouled below design performance.

Log mean temperature difference

LMTD = (ΔT₁ − ΔT₂) ÷ ln(ΔT₁ ÷ ΔT₂)

ΔT₁ = temperature difference at one end · ΔT₂ = temperature difference at the other end

For counterflow, ΔT₁ = Thot,in − Tcold,out and ΔT₂ = Thot,out − Tcold,in. When ΔT₁ ≈ ΔT₂ the log term approaches zero — use the arithmetic mean instead to avoid a divide-by-zero.

Overall coefficient from individual resistances

1/U = 1/hi + Rfi + x/k + Rfo + 1/ho

hi, ho = inside/outside film coefficients · Rfi, Rfo = fouling factors · x = wall thickness · k = wall thermal conductivity

Resistances add in series. In industrial service fouling is often the dominant term — which is why filtration and water treatment have such a direct effect on exchanger performance.

Effectiveness–NTU

ε = Qactual ÷ Qmax  |  NTU = U·A ÷ Cmin

ε = effectiveness (0–1) · Cmin = smaller of the two ṁ·cp products

Useful when outlet temperatures are unknown, where LMTD would require iteration. Qmax = Cmin · (Thot,in − Tcold,in).

Cooling tower performance

Range

Range = Thot water in − Tcold water out

Set by the process heat load and circulating flow rate, not by the tower itself. A tower doesn’t determine range — the system does.

Approach

Approach = Tcold water out − Tambient wet bulb

The true measure of tower capability. Approach cannot reach zero at any finite size — the wet bulb is the thermodynamic floor. Tighter approaches drive size and cost up sharply below about 7 °F.

Tower efficiency

η = Range ÷ (Range + Approach) × 100%

A quick relative indicator, most useful for comparing operating condition against design rather than comparing different towers to each other.

Liquid-to-gas ratio

L/G = ṁwater ÷ ṁair

A key design parameter for fill selection and fan sizing. Higher L/G means more water per unit of air, generally requiring more fill or airflow to hold the same approach.

Water balance

Evaporation rate

E ≈ 0.001 · GPM · Range

E = evaporation (GPM) · Range = cooling range (°F)

The common rule of thumb: roughly 1% of circulating flow evaporates per 10 °F of range. Actual evaporation varies with ambient conditions — this is for preliminary water-use budgeting, not permitting submittals.

Cycles of concentration

COC = TDScirculating ÷ TDSmakeup

Can equivalently be calculated from conductivity or chloride ratios. Higher cycles reduce water and chemical use but increase scaling and corrosion risk — the practical ceiling is set by makeup water chemistry.

Blowdown rate

B = E ÷ (COC − 1) − D

B = blowdown (GPM) · E = evaporation (GPM) · D = drift loss (GPM)

Drift is typically small in modern towers with effective eliminators (often below 0.005% of circulating flow) but should not be ignored on older equipment.

Makeup water

M = E + B + D

Total makeup equals everything leaving the system. Useful for sizing makeup lines and estimating annual water cost.

Flow, pressure & power

Pump brake horsepower

BHP = (GPM · H · SG) ÷ (3960 · η)

H = total head (ft) · SG = specific gravity · η = pump efficiency (decimal)

The 3960 constant converts to horsepower for water. Multiply by motor efficiency to get electrical input power for energy cost estimates.

Fan brake horsepower

BHP = (CFM · SP) ÷ (6356 · η)

SP = static pressure (in. w.g.) · η = fan efficiency (decimal)

Fan energy is a substantial share of cooling tower and air-cooled exchanger operating cost — worth evaluating during selection, not just at first cost.

Affinity laws

Q₂/Q₁ = N₂/N₁  |  H₂/H₁ = (N₂/N₁)²  |  P₂/P₁ = (N₂/N₁)³

Q = flow · H = head/pressure · P = power · N = speed

The cube relationship on power is why variable speed drives pay back quickly on pumps and fans: a 20% speed reduction cuts power roughly in half.

Reynolds number

Re = (ρ · V · D) ÷ μ

ρ = density · V = velocity · D = characteristic diameter · μ = dynamic viscosity

Below roughly 2,300 flow is laminar; above roughly 4,000 it is turbulent. Turbulent flow substantially improves heat transfer — one reason low-flow operation hurts exchanger performance disproportionately.

Engineering note: these relationships are provided as a general reference for preliminary work. Final equipment selection should account for site-specific conditions, fluid properties, fouling allowances, and applicable code requirements. Contact our team to review a specific application.

Notes on the calculations

Fluid properties

Specific gravity, specific heat, freeze point, and pressure-drop factor are interpolated from published glycol solution data at typical loop temperatures. Properties shift with temperature — treat them as design-point approximations.

Evaporative vs. dry

Evaporative equipment is limited by ambient wet bulb; dry equipment is limited by dry bulb. The tool validates against the correct limit for the equipment selected and will not return a physically impossible approach.

Exchanger sizing

Duty comes from the hot-side heat balance; the cold-side outlet is calculated from it. MTD uses counterflow LMTD with a TEMA F-correction applied for 1-2 shell-and-tube configurations.

Cooling equipment loses efficiency gradually — scaling, fouling, and mechanical wear all chip away at performance long before a failure shows up. Our performance evaluations measure your equipment against its original design specifications, identifying efficiency losses and giving you data to plan repairs, upgrades, or replacement on your own timeline rather than in an emergency.

A fouled exchanger shows up as a drop in the overall coefficient U — see the resistance equation for how fouling factors into total performance.
